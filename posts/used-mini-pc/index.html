Used-gear hunting: how to buy a secondhand mini PC without regret

A small used computer can be a smart buy for the front desk, a shared office PC, or a first try at running AI on your own machine. Here's how to buy one without regret.

If you're buying for local AI, memory matters most. We covered that in what fits on a 64 GB Mac mini and what a 2022 Mac Studio can run. This post is about the buying part.

Before you buy: read the listing like a skeptic

1. The return window. Find it before you look at anything else. The FTC says some marketplaces leave returns and refunds entirely up to the seller, and even when a site helps, "there may be a short window of time" to return the item or claim a refund (FTC). No returns? Think hard before you buy.

2. Specs as listed vs. specs as shown. The FTC suggests looking closely at the photos. If all you see are stock photos, you can't check the item's condition or be sure the seller has it (FTC). Ask for a photo of the actual machine's spec screen. Compare it with what the listing says.

3. Memory and storage. Write down the exact amounts the listing promises. You'll check them on arrival. For Windows 11, Microsoft's minimum is 4 GB of memory and 64 GB of storage (Microsoft). That's a floor, not a goal.

4. Ports. Count the ports you need: monitors, keyboard, network cable, USB drives. Then count them in the photos.

5. The power adapter. Ask if it's in the box. If the model uses a separate power brick, ask if it's the original one.

6. The operating system and its license. Microsoft stopped security updates for Windows 10 on October 14, 2025 (Microsoft). Windows 11 needs, among other things, a TPM 2.0 chip and a processor on Microsoft's approved list (Microsoft). Ask which version it runs and whether it's activated. If you buy a refurbished PC, Microsoft says the refurbisher, not Microsoft, must give you a product key (Microsoft).

7. The account lock (Macs). On recent Macs, turning on Find My turns on Activation Lock. Then nobody can erase the Mac or set it up again without the owner's Apple Account password or passcode (Apple). Ask the seller to sign out and remove it from their account before it ships.

8. Warranty transfer. Some makers move leftover warranty to a new owner, but you have to ask. Dell, for example, wants the previous owner's details plus the Service Tag and Express Code, and says a domestic transfer takes 7 to 10 business days (Dell). For a Mac, ask for the serial number and enter it at Apple's checkcoverage.apple.com (Apple).

Want the least guesswork? Apple Certified Refurbished Macs come with Apple's one-year limited warranty, plus free shipping and returns (Apple).

Red flags: walk away

  • Odd payment requests. The FTC says don't pay by wire transfer, gift cards, reload cards or crypto, and don't buy from anyone who wants payment outside the marketplace. Use a credit card or a secure payment system that protects buyers (FTC).
  • A price that's too good. A very low price on an expensive item may mean it's fake (FTC).
  • "As is." The FTC notes that words like "used," "refurbished" or "as is" mean it probably isn't in top condition (FTC). Price it that way.
  • A Mac still on someone's account. If the seller can't remove Activation Lock, you'll need proof of purchase to ask Apple for help (Apple).
  • Vague answers. Get answers in writing and keep copies of your messages (FTC).

The day it arrives: test before the window closes

Put the return deadline on your calendar first. The FTC says to keep track of it (FTC). Then:

  1. Check the specs. On Windows, open Settings, then System, then About. It shows the processor and the amount of memory installed. Storage is under System, then Storage (Microsoft). On a Mac, open System Settings, then General, then About. Click Details for warranty coverage, or System Report for the installed hardware (Apple).
  2. Check activation. On Windows 11, go to Settings, then System, then Activation (Microsoft).
  3. Run the built-in hardware test on a Mac. Apple Diagnostics helps find which part might be at fault. On Apple silicon, hold the power button until you see Options, then hold Command-D (Apple).
  4. Plug everything in. Every port, your monitor, your network cable. Use the power adapter that came with it.
  5. Start fresh. Don't trust the last owner's setup. On Windows, use Reset this PC with "Remove everything" (Microsoft). On a Mac, use Erase All Content and Settings. It needs macOS Monterey or later and an Apple silicon or T2 Mac (Apple). Then confirm it still activates.
  6. Start the warranty transfer if the maker offers one.

If anything doesn't match the listing, message the seller that day, in writing. Buy it like you might need to send it back.
