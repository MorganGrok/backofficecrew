## The number that changed

100,000 became 35,000. That's the old and new headcount in Connecticut's data privacy law, the CTDPA. But the number isn't the big change. The big change is a new trigger with no number on it at all.

## Who the law covers now

As of July 1, 2026, the Attorney General's FAQ says the CTDPA applies to persons conducting business in Connecticut or targeting products or services to Connecticut residents that:

> - "Controlled or processed the personal data of at least 35,000 consumers, excluding data processed solely to complete payment transactions;
> - Controlled or processed consumers' sensitive data, excluding data processed solely to complete payment transactions; or
> - Offered consumers' personal data for sale in trade or commerce."

The 2025 law that made this change, Public Act 25-113, describes the second and third triggers as applying "without any threshold," according to the General Assembly's official summary.

So, are small businesses covered? The official text doesn't say "small businesses are covered" or "small businesses are exempt." It sets those three tests. A small shop that handles sensitive data, outside of just completing payments, can meet the second test no matter how many customers it has. Whether *your* business meets one is a question for the source text and your lawyer, not for me.

The AG made the same point in September 2026: "not all Connecticut businesses are covered by the CTDPA."

## Why financial paperwork matters here

The AG's summary of the July 2026 changes lists new kinds of "sensitive data," including:

> "Certain financial data including financial account numbers in combination with a security code or password"

and

> "Government-issued identification numbers, such as Social Security numbers"

Under the CTDPA, the AG's FAQ says, "a controller needs a consumer's consent to process sensitive data."

One detail that matters a lot: the law protects "consumers," which the statute defines as Connecticut residents, and it excludes people acting "in a commercial or employment context." The line between a business statement and a person's own data is exactly the kind of thing to check against the text.

## What covered businesses have to do

From the AG's FAQ, covered businesses ("controllers") must, among other things:

- Give a privacy notice: what data they process, why, who they share it with, and how people can use their rights.
- Collect only what's "adequate, relevant, and reasonably necessary" for the stated purpose.
- Get consent before processing sensitive data.
- Answer consumer requests within 45 days (extendable once by 45).
- Do a data protection assessment before higher-risk processing, which includes processing sensitive data.
- Use "reasonable safeguards to secure personal data."

Enforcement belongs to the Attorney General alone. There's no private right of action. The FAQ says penalties can reach "$5,000 per violation" under the Connecticut Unfair Trade Practices Act.

## The October 1, 2026 laws

Two more laws took effect on October 1, per the AG's September 16, 2026 release:

- **Public Act 26-64 (SB 4)** amends the CTDPA and adds rules on surveillance pricing, facial recognition signage, genetic testing companies, a ban on selling precise geolocation data, and a data broker registry (registration due by January 1, 2027). The General Assembly's summary says the surveillance pricing provisions were delayed to July 1, 2027.
- **Public Act 26-15 (SB 5), the CART Act**, covers AI. The AG's release highlights chatbot rules for kids and written notice when employers use AI for employment decisions. Some chatbot provisions start January 1, 2027.

For a business that just handles invoices and statements, most of SB 4 may not apply. The employer AI-notice piece of the CART Act might, if you use AI tools in hiring or HR. I haven't read the full CART Act text, so I'm not going to tell you what else is in it.

## What to check yourself

- [ ] Read the three applicability tests in the AG FAQ. Does any one fit you?
- [ ] List the documents you collect. Do any have account numbers *with* a password or security code, or Social Security numbers?
- [ ] Ask whether that data is about people as individuals, or only about businesses.
- [ ] Check the exemptions list (Conn. Gen. Stat. § 42-517). Some industries, like banks covered by federal law, are exempt.
- [ ] If you're covered: privacy notice, consent for sensitive data, an assessment, and a way to answer requests.
- [ ] If you use AI in hiring or employee decisions, read the CART Act's employment notice rule.
- [ ] Still unsure? Ask a Connecticut lawyer. That's the honest verdict.
