"Check the sender" is good advice. With this scam, it doesn't help, because the email really does come from Google.

## How the trick works

Switzerland's National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) described it on September 22. A scammer makes their own Google account and lists your email address as its recovery address. Google sends security notices about an account to its recovery address. The scammer then creates an "app password" on their account and types their own message into the name box. Google's real security alert copies that text in, so the email looks like Google is running a support case, with a staff name and a case number.

The University of Michigan saw the same thing on October 6. There, the scam text was a fake $399 McAfee invoice and a support phone number. The email carried valid signatures and passed every check the university's mail system ran. The links in it went to the reader's own clean Google dashboard, which explains nothing. That leaves one thing to do: call the number. The person who answers is the scammer.

In the Swiss case, a caller posing as Google support phoned the victim soon after the email arrived and pushed them to a fake sign-in page. The NCSC's advice is simple: real platforms never call you out of the blue about a security problem. If they do, hang up. Google says it never asks for your password in an email, message or phone call.

## The 60-second check

If one of these lands in your inbox, or a staff member forwards one to you:

1. **Don't call the number in the email, and don't reply.** Google's own advice is not to answer suspicious emails or calls that ask for your personal or financial details.
2. **Type myaccount.google.com into your browser yourself.** Don't click through from the email.
3. **Look at your security activity.** Open your [Recent security activity](https://myaccount.google.com/notifications) page. Then go to Security & sign-in, and under "Your devices," select Manage all devices. Look for sign-ins or devices you don't recognize.
4. **Delete app passwords you don't recognize.** Open your App Passwords list and select Remove next to any you didn't set up. Google says app passwords aren't recommended and are unnecessary in most cases, so many accounts won't have any. Google also notes the option may not appear on some work or school accounts.

If everything looks normal, delete the email. The scammer sent it by changing settings on their own Google account, not yours.

## If something looks wrong

Google's steps for a suspected break-in are to change your password right away, then sign out of any device you don't recognize. Then check your recovery phone and email, and your automatic mail forwarding, for changes you didn't make. The Swiss NCSC gives the same advice.
